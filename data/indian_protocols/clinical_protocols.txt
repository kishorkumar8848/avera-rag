Clinical Protocol for Anaemia:
Presentation: For patients (especially women, children, and pregnant women) presenting with paleness of skin/nails/inner eyelids, unusual tiredness, weakness, breathlessness on exertion, dizziness, or fast heartbeat.
1. Home Care: Advise iron- and folate-rich foods (green leafy vegetables, jaggery, dates, eggs, meat, pulses). Give Iron-Folic Acid (IFA) tablets as per the national supplementation schedule for the patient's age group (child, adolescent, or pregnant woman). Deworm as per schedule, since worm infestation is a common cause of anaemia. Advise vitamin C-rich foods (citrus fruits, amla) alongside iron intake to improve absorption, and avoid tea/coffee immediately after meals.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer to a doctor or PHC for haemoglobin testing if pallor is severe, if there is breathlessness at rest, swelling of feet, fainting spells, blood in stool/urine, or if the patient is pregnant with symptoms of severe anaemia. Any anaemia not improving after 2–3 months of supplementation needs medical evaluation for an underlying cause.

Clinical Protocol for Sickle Cell Disease:
Presentation: In known or suspected sickle cell patients (common in tribal populations) presenting with sudden severe pain in bones, joints, chest, abdomen, or back (a 'pain crisis'), unusual paleness, yellowing of eyes, fatigue, or repeated infections/fever.
1. Home Care: During a mild pain episode, keep the patient warm, give plenty of oral fluids, and give Paracetamol for pain relief. Encourage rest and avoid extreme cold, dehydration, high altitude, or overexertion, all of which can trigger a crisis. Ensure the patient carries their sickle cell status card/identification. Encourage routine folic acid supplementation and follow-up at the sickle cell clinic if enrolled under the national program.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer immediately to a doctor, PHC, or the nearest sickle cell/hospital facility for severe or worsening pain not relieved by Paracetamol, high fever, chest pain or difficulty breathing, severe abdominal pain, sudden weakness/numbness on one side of the body, priapism (painful prolonged erection in males), severe paleness, or swelling of hands/feet in a young child (may indicate a crisis requiring hospital-based pain management, fluids, or transfusion).

Clinical Protocol for Lymphatic Filariasis (LF) / Elephantiasis:
Presentation: For patients presenting with recurrent episodes of fever with chills, painful swelling and redness along the arm or leg (acute filarial lymphangitis), or long-standing swelling/thickening of the leg, arm, breast, or scrotum (hydrocele) that has developed gradually over months to years.
1. Home Care: During an acute attack: rest, elevate the swollen limb, apply cool compresses, and give Paracetamol for fever and pain. For chronic swelling (lymphoedema): teach and encourage daily limb hygiene — wash the limb with soap and water, dry gently between toes/skin folds, apply antiseptic cream to any small cuts or fungal patches, keep nails trimmed, wear footwear at all times to prevent injury and entry-point infection, elevate the limb at night, and perform gentle prescribed exercises to aid lymph drainage. Ensure participation in the annual Mass Drug Administration (MDA) programme (DEC + Albendazole) in endemic areas, if not contraindicated.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer to a doctor or PHC for confirmation and MDA eligibility screening if this is a new case, for hydrocele needing surgical evaluation, for a limb with spreading redness/high fever/foul discharge (suggesting acute infection of an already swollen limb), or for guidance on Morbidity Management and Disability Prevention (MMDP) services for chronic lymphoedema.

Clinical Protocol for Snake Bite:
Presentation: For any patient with a history of snake bite, with or without visible fang marks, who may show local swelling/pain at the bite site, or systemic signs such as drooping eyelids, blurred/double vision, difficulty swallowing or speaking, breathlessness, bleeding from gums/nose, or dark urine.
1. Home Care: Reassure and keep the patient calm and still to slow venom spread. Remove rings, watches, or tight clothing near the bite before swelling starts. Immobilize the bitten limb with a splint, keeping it at or slightly below heart level. Do NOT cut, suck, or apply a tight tourniquet to the wound; do NOT apply ice, herbs, chemicals, or electric shock; do NOT give any oral medication, alcohol, or attempt to catch/kill the snake unnecessarily. If safe and possible, note the snake's colour/pattern or take a photograph from a safe distance to help identification at the hospital.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Every snake bite is a medical emergency — transport the patient immediately to the nearest hospital or PHC with anti-snake venom (ASV) availability, even if the patient appears well, since severe symptoms can develop after a delay. Treat it as most urgent if there is drooping eyelids, difficulty breathing/swallowing, spontaneous bleeding, dark urine, severe local swelling spreading rapidly, or loss of consciousness.

Clinical Protocol for Leprosy (Hansen's Disease):
Presentation: For patients presenting with one or more pale or reddish patches on the skin with definite loss of sensation (numbness to touch, heat, or pain) in that patch, thickened or tender nerves (commonly near the elbow, wrist, knee, or behind the ear), or weakness/clawing of fingers or foot-drop without another obvious cause.
1. Home Care: Reassure the patient that leprosy is fully curable with Multi-Drug Therapy (MDT) available free of cost at PHCs, and that early treatment prevents disability. Encourage the patient to continue MDT for the full prescribed duration even after skin patches start to fade, since stopping early can lead to relapse or resistance. Advise care of numb hands/feet — inspect daily for injuries or burns (which may go unnoticed due to numbness), soak and gently file thickened skin, and use protective footwear.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer to the PHC or district leprosy/skin (dermatology) clinic for diagnosis confirmation and initiation of MDT if leprosy is suspected. Refer urgently if there is sudden nerve pain/tenderness, new or worsening weakness in hands/feet, red painful skin nodules with fever (possible leprosy reaction), or non-healing wounds/ulcers on numb areas.

Clinical Protocol for Kala-azar (Visceral Leishmaniasis) / PKDL:
Presentation: For patients in endemic areas presenting with prolonged fever (more than 2 weeks, often with two peaks a day), progressive weakness, significant weight loss, and a distended abdomen due to enlargement of the spleen and/or liver. For PKDL, look for pale, reddish, or hypopigmented skin patches/nodules on the face and body, often in a person previously treated for kala-azar.
1. Home Care: While awaiting referral, ensure the patient is well hydrated and nourished with energy-dense, protein-rich foods to counter weight loss. Give Paracetamol for fever. Encourage use of insecticide-treated bed nets at night and support indoor residual spraying activities in the household, since sandfly bites transmit the disease.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer any patient with prolonged unexplained fever with abdominal swelling/spleen enlargement to the PHC or district hospital for kala-azar testing (rK39 test) and treatment — this cannot be managed at home. Refer any suspicious chronic skin patches in a person with a past kala-azar history for PKDL evaluation. Refer immediately if there is severe paleness, bleeding from gums/nose, jaundice, or breathing difficulty, which can indicate advanced disease.

Clinical Protocol for Scrub Typhus:
Presentation: For patients in forest/scrub/tall-grass areas presenting with sudden high fever, severe headache, body aches, and — importantly — a painless black scab-like sore (eschar) at the site of a mite bite, often in the armpit, groin, or waistline, sometimes with a rash and swollen lymph nodes.
1. Home Care: Give Paracetamol for fever and body aches and ensure the patient stays well hydrated. Keep the bite area/eschar clean and covered. Encourage wearing full-sleeved clothing tucked into socks/pants and using insect repellent when working in bushy or scrub areas to prevent further mite bites.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer promptly to a doctor or PHC for any fever lasting more than 2–3 days, especially with a visible eschar, rash, or swollen lymph nodes, as scrub typhus requires specific antibiotic treatment (e.g., Doxycycline) started early. Refer immediately if there is breathlessness, confusion, yellowing of eyes, reduced urine output, or bleeding, as untreated scrub typhus can rapidly worsen and affect the lungs, kidneys, or brain.

Clinical Protocol for Malaria:
Presentation: For patients presenting with intermittent high fever with chills and rigors, sweating, headache, body ache, nausea/vomiting, and fatigue, particularly in mosquito-endemic or forested/tribal areas.
1. Home Care: Give Paracetamol for fever and encourage plenty of fluids and rest. Arrange for a rapid diagnostic test (RDT) or blood smear at the earliest, available at most sub-centres/PHCs/ASHA kits, since malaria treatment depends on confirming the species. Encourage use of insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor residual spraying, and elimination of stagnant water around the house to prevent further transmission.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer to a PHC for testing and appropriate antimalarial treatment as soon as malaria is suspected — do not treat blindly with leftover medicines. Refer immediately/urgently (possible severe/cerebral malaria) if there is high fever with confusion, drowsiness, repeated vomiting, convulsions, yellowing of eyes, dark (cola-coloured) urine, breathlessness, or inability to drink fluids, especially in young children and pregnant women.

Clinical Protocol for Dengue Fever:
Presentation: For patients presenting with sudden high fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, muscle and joint pain, skin rash, and mild bleeding tendencies (gum bleeding, easy bruising), typically during or after the monsoon season.
1. Home Care: Give Paracetamol only for fever and pain — strictly avoid Aspirin, Ibuprofen, or other NSAIDs, as these increase bleeding risk. Encourage generous oral fluids (ORS, coconut water, fruit juice) to maintain hydration. Advise complete rest and use of mosquito nets/repellents to prevent further Aedes mosquito bites and spread to others in the household.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer to a doctor or hospital for platelet count monitoring, especially between day 3–7 of fever, when complications are most likely. Refer immediately/emergency if there is severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding from gums/nose/in vomit or stool, cold clammy skin, restlessness or lethargy, difficulty breathing, or a sudden drop in fever accompanied by these warning signs (suggests dengue shock/haemorrhagic complications).

Clinical Protocol for Chikungunya:
Presentation: For patients presenting with sudden high fever accompanied by severe, often disabling, joint pain and swelling (commonly hands, wrists, ankles, knees), muscle pain, headache, and sometimes a skin rash.
1. Home Care: Give Paracetamol for fever and joint pain (avoid Aspirin/NSAIDs until dengue is ruled out, as symptoms overlap). Encourage rest, adequate fluids, and gentle movement of joints once acute pain reduces to prevent stiffness. Use mosquito repellents/nets, as the same mosquito spreads both chikungunya and dengue.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer to a doctor or PHC if fever persists beyond 3–5 days, if joint pain is severe and disabling, or to rule out dengue/other causes of fever with joint pain. Refer urgently if there is bleeding, severe abdominal pain, confusion, or breathing difficulty, or if a very young infant, elderly person, or someone with other chronic illness is affected.

Clinical Protocol for Tuberculosis (Pulmonary TB):
Presentation: For patients presenting with cough lasting more than 2 weeks, fever (often low-grade and evening/night), unintentional weight loss, loss of appetite, night sweats, or blood in sputum.
1. Home Care: Advise the patient to cover the mouth while coughing/sneezing and, where possible, sleep in a separate well-ventilated room to reduce household spread until diagnosis and initial treatment. Encourage a nutritious, protein-rich diet. If already diagnosed and on treatment (DOTS), stress strict adherence to the full course without interruption, since incomplete treatment causes drug resistance and relapse — link the patient to the free national TB programme and nutritional support (Ni-kshay) benefits.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer any person with cough for more than 2 weeks to the PHC/TB unit for free sputum testing (and chest X-ray if needed) without delay. Refer urgently for coughing up blood, severe breathlessness, chest pain, or high fever with drowsiness. Also screen and refer close household contacts of a diagnosed TB patient, especially children, for testing.

Clinical Protocol for Typhoid Fever:
Presentation: For patients presenting with prolonged (stepladder-pattern) fever lasting more than a week, headache, abdominal discomfort, constipation or diarrhea, loss of appetite, and general weakness, often linked to contaminated food or water.
1. Home Care: Give Paracetamol for fever. Encourage soft, easily digestible, high-calorie foods and plenty of safe (boiled/filtered) fluids. Emphasize drinking only boiled or treated water and maintaining strict hand hygiene, especially before eating and after using the toilet, to prevent spread to others.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer to a doctor or PHC for blood testing and antibiotic treatment for any fever persisting beyond 5–7 days. Refer immediately if there is severe abdominal pain or distension, rigidity of the abdomen, blood in stool, persistent vomiting, drowsiness/confusion, or signs of severe dehydration, which may indicate intestinal complications.

Clinical Protocol for Jaundice / Viral Hepatitis:
Presentation: For patients presenting with yellowing of the eyes and skin, dark-coloured urine, pale stools, loss of appetite, nausea, fatigue, and mild right upper abdominal discomfort.
1. Home Care: Advise complete rest, a light and low-fat diet, and plenty of safe fluids. Strictly avoid alcohol and unnecessary medicines, especially Paracetamol overuse and unregulated herbal/'liver tonic' preparations, which can worsen liver injury. Emphasize safe drinking water, proper handwashing, and hygienic food handling to prevent spread within the household (for hepatitis A/E).
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer to a doctor or PHC for testing to identify the cause and to rule out complications, especially in pregnant women, in whom viral hepatitis can be more severe. Refer immediately if there is confusion or altered behaviour, excessive drowsiness, persistent vomiting, bleeding tendencies, marked abdominal swelling, or reduced urine output, which suggest liver failure.

Clinical Protocol for Measles:
Presentation: For children presenting with high fever, cough, runny nose, red watery eyes (conjunctivitis), followed by a characteristic red-brown rash that starts on the face and spreads downward to the body.
1. Home Care: Give Paracetamol for fever and encourage plenty of fluids and continued feeding, including breastfeeding in infants. Give Vitamin A supplementation as per national guidelines if advised at the health facility. Isolate the child from other unvaccinated children as measles spreads very easily, and keep the room well-ventilated.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer to a doctor or PHC if the fever is very high or persists beyond 3–4 days after the rash appears, or for Vitamin A dosing if not already given. Refer immediately for difficulty breathing, persistent diarrhea/vomiting causing dehydration, ear discharge, drowsiness, convulsions, or eye discharge with pain (risk of corneal damage), all of which suggest complications requiring urgent care.

Clinical Protocol for Chickenpox:
Presentation: For patients presenting with mild fever, tiredness, and an itchy rash that progresses from red spots to fluid-filled blisters and then crusted scabs, appearing in crops over several days, typically starting on the trunk/face.
1. Home Care: Give Paracetamol for fever (avoid Aspirin). Keep nails trimmed and encourage the patient not to scratch, to prevent secondary bacterial infection and scarring; calamine lotion can soothe itching. Keep the patient isolated from pregnant women, newborns, and immunocompromised individuals until all blisters have crusted over, and maintain light, loose clothing.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer to a doctor or PHC if fever is high or prolonged, if blisters become increasingly red, swollen, warm, or pus-filled (secondary infection), or if the patient is a pregnant woman, newborn, or has a weakened immune system. Refer immediately for difficulty breathing, persistent vomiting, severe headache with neck stiffness, or extreme drowsiness/confusion.

Clinical Protocol for Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) / Malnutrition in Children:
Presentation: For children presenting with visible severe wasting (thin arms/legs, loose skin folds), bilateral pitting oedema of both feet, very low weight-for-height/age, poor appetite, lethargy, or a mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) below the danger threshold for age.
1. Home Care: For children without danger signs, counsel caregivers on frequent small feeds of energy- and nutrient-dense, age-appropriate food, continued breastfeeding for infants/toddlers, and use of take-home rations or supplementary nutrition provided under the local Anganwadi/ICDS programme. Ensure the child's immunizations and deworming are up to date and follow up regularly at the Anganwadi centre for growth monitoring.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer immediately to a Nutrition Rehabilitation Centre (NRC), PHC, or hospital for any child with bilateral pitting oedema, visible severe wasting, MUAC in the red zone, poor appetite/refusal to feed, lethargy or reduced consciousness, fever, or diarrhea/vomiting in a malnourished child — these children need close medical supervision and cannot be safely managed at home alone.

Clinical Protocol for Scabies:
Presentation: For patients (often multiple family members) presenting with intense itching that worsens at night, and small red bumps or burrow lines especially between fingers, wrists, elbows, armpits, waistline, and genital area.
1. Home Care: Advise treating all household/close contacts at the same time to prevent reinfection, even if not all are symptomatic. Apply prescribed anti-scabies lotion (e.g., Permethrin) as directed to the whole body from neck down, left on for the recommended duration, then washed off. Wash all clothing, bedding, and towels in hot water and dry in sunlight; avoid sharing personal items.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer to a PHC or doctor for confirmation and to obtain the correct topical treatment, especially for infants, pregnant women, or widespread/crusted lesions. Refer if there is spreading redness, swelling, pus, or fever at the itchy sites, suggesting secondary bacterial skin infection.

Clinical Protocol for Intestinal Worm Infestation:
Presentation: For children or adults presenting with recurrent abdominal pain, bloating, poor appetite, visible worms in stool, itching around the anus (especially at night), or unexplained anaemia/poor growth in children.
1. Home Care: Give deworming tablets (e.g., Albendazole) as per the national deworming programme schedule for children and adolescents. Emphasize handwashing with soap before eating and after defecation, wearing footwear outdoors, drinking safe water, and proper use of toilets/sanitation to prevent reinfection.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer to a PHC if there is persistent abdominal pain despite deworming, visible blood in stool, significant weight loss, or signs of anaemia. Refer immediately for severe abdominal pain with vomiting and abdominal distension, or inability to pass stool/gas, which may indicate a worm-related intestinal obstruction.

Clinical Protocol for Asthma / Chronic Respiratory Disease:
Presentation: For patients with known or suspected asthma/chronic airway disease presenting with wheeze, chest tightness, cough (often worse at night or with exertion/cold air/smoke), and shortness of breath.
1. Home Care: Help the patient sit upright in a comfortable, well-ventilated position and use their prescribed reliever inhaler if available. Help identify and reduce exposure to known triggers such as smoke (including chulha/biomass cooking smoke), dust, and cold air. Ensure the patient continues any prescribed regular (preventer) medication even when feeling well.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer to a doctor or PHC for a new diagnosis, for adjustment of long-term treatment, or if attacks are becoming more frequent or severe despite inhaler use. Refer immediately/emergency for severe breathlessness where the patient cannot speak full sentences, blue discolouration of lips/fingertips, drowsiness, or no relief after using the reliever inhaler.

Clinical Protocol for Epilepsy / Seizure Disorder:
Presentation: For patients with a history of, or a witnessed, seizure — sudden loss of consciousness with jerking movements of the limbs/body, staring spells, or brief loss of awareness.
1. Home Care: During a seizure: stay calm, protect the person from injury by clearing the surrounding area, gently turn them onto their side (recovery position), and place something soft under the head. Do NOT put anything in the mouth, do NOT restrain the limbs forcefully, and do NOT give food/water until fully alert. Time the seizure. Afterward, allow the person to rest and reorient. Encourage strict adherence to prescribed anti-seizure medication for known epilepsy patients — sudden stopping can trigger seizures.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer any first-time seizure to a doctor or PHC for evaluation. Refer immediately/emergency if a seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes, if seizures repeat one after another without the person regaining consciousness in between, if there is injury, high fever with the seizure (especially in a child), difficulty breathing after the seizure, or if the person does not regain consciousness within a reasonable time.

Clinical Protocol for Rabies Risk (Animal / Dog Bite):
Presentation: For any patient with a bite, scratch, or lick on broken skin/mucous membrane from a dog, cat, monkey, or other mammal, particularly a stray or unvaccinated/behaviourally abnormal animal.
1. Home Care: Immediately and thoroughly wash the wound with soap and running water for at least 15 minutes — this single step significantly reduces infection risk. Apply an antiseptic (e.g., povidone-iodine) after washing. Do NOT apply chilli powder, oil, herbs, or other traditional substances to the wound, and do NOT suture the wound immediately unless medically necessary.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer every animal bite case to a PHC or Anti-Rabies Clinic without delay for wound assessment and to start the anti-rabies vaccination schedule (and rabies immunoglobulin for severe/category III bites), regardless of how minor the bite appears — rabies is fatal once symptoms start, so post-exposure treatment should never be skipped or delayed.

Clinical Protocol for Scorpion Sting:
Presentation: For patients presenting with severe local pain, redness, and swelling at the sting site, sometimes progressing to sweating, restlessness, vomiting, drooling, muscle twitching, or fast heartbeat, particularly after a sting from a red/black endemic scorpion species.
1. Home Care: Keep the patient calm and still, and wash the sting site with soap and water. Apply a cold compress to the site to reduce pain and swelling. Give Paracetamol for pain if needed. Do NOT apply a tourniquet, cut the wound, or apply herbal pastes.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer immediately to a doctor, PHC, or hospital for any scorpion sting with severe pain not controlled by simple measures, or with systemic symptoms such as sweating, restlessness, vomiting, drooling, breathlessness, fast/irregular heartbeat, or signs of shock — these may need specific antivenom and monitoring, especially in young children.

Clinical Protocol for Goitre / Iodine Deficiency Disorder:
Presentation: For patients presenting with a visible or palpable swelling in the front of the neck (thyroid gland enlargement), sometimes with fatigue, weight changes, or, in children born to iodine-deficient mothers, delayed growth or learning difficulties.
1. Home Care: Advise consistent use of iodized salt for all household cooking and storage of salt in a dry, airtight container away from direct heat/sunlight (iodine content reduces with improper storage). Encourage inclusion of iodine-rich foods where available (e.g., seafood, dairy, eggs) as a supplement to iodized salt.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer to a PHC or doctor for evaluation of any new or enlarging neck swelling, or for symptoms of thyroid dysfunction (unexplained weight change, palpitations, heat/cold intolerance, tremor). Refer urgently if the swelling causes difficulty breathing or swallowing, or grows rapidly.

Clinical Protocol for Danger Signs in Pregnancy:
Presentation: For any pregnant woman presenting with symptoms outside the routine discomforts of pregnancy — severe headache, blurred vision, swelling of face/hands, vaginal bleeding, reduced or absent fetal movements, high fever, severe abdominal pain, or leaking of fluid from the vagina.
1. Home Care: Ensure the woman rests in a calm environment and stays hydrated while arrangements for transport are made. Do not attempt to manage any of the danger signs listed below at home — this list exists specifically to identify pregnancies that need facility-based care without delay. Continue routine antenatal supplements (IFA, calcium) as already prescribed if the woman is otherwise stable.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer immediately to a facility with obstetric care (PHC/CHC/hospital) for any vaginal bleeding at any stage of pregnancy, severe headache with blurred vision or swelling (possible pre-eclampsia), reduced/absent fetal movements, high fever, watery vaginal discharge (possible ruptured membranes), severe abdominal pain, convulsions, or prolonged labour — these are obstetric emergencies where delay can be life-threatening for mother and baby.

Clinical Protocol for Acute Encephalitis Syndrome (including Japanese Encephalitis):
Presentation: For patients, especially children in endemic rural/agricultural areas, presenting with fever together with a change in mental state — confusion, disorientation, excessive drowsiness, irritability — or new-onset seizures.
1. Home Care: This condition cannot be managed at home. While arranging urgent transport, keep the patient safe from injury (especially if drowsy or seizing, use the seizure first-aid steps above), keep them cool if febrile, and do not force feed or give oral fluids if the patient is not fully alert (risk of choking). Encourage JE vaccination as per the national immunization schedule and use of mosquito nets/repellents in endemic areas as prevention.
2. Referral (Red Flags): Refer immediately and urgently — this is a medical emergency. Any fever with altered consciousness, confusion, seizures, or inability to wake a child normally must be transported to the nearest hospital/PHC without any delay, as encephalitis can progress rapidly and cause permanent disability or death if untreated.
